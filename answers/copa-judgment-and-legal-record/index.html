What did the COPA judgment establish—and what does it not decide?

“COPA proved fraud and forgery. Any BSV technical claim is therefore discredited.”
Short answer

The answer without the theater.

The High Court's approved judgment found that Craig Wright was not Satoshi Nakamoto, did not author the Bitcoin whitepaper, did not create the Bitcoin system, and did not operate under the Satoshi pseudonym. That record should be stated accurately, not relitigated by this site. It also does not answer whether a specific wallet interface, proof, node rule, or application works.

What is true

The objection's strongest ground

  • The approved judgment is the authoritative starting point for the High Court's findings, not social-media summaries.
  • The identity campaign and evidence discussed by the court created a durable trust problem for the broader ecosystem.
  • A technical claim still requires evidence matched to that claim: source, tests, measurements, or a reproducible flow.
What remains unresolved

Do not claim more than the evidence

  • This brief is not a complete procedural history and does not substitute for reading the judgment and later orders.
  • Separating scope does not excuse anyone from disclosing relevant relationships or history.
Inspect it yourself

A better next move than arguing

  1. Read the court's published summary and approved judgment before repeating a quotation or characterization.
  2. For any separate claim, ask what evidence would prove it if no identity dispute existed.
  3. Correct ecosystem material that still presents Wright's identity claim as a technical premise.
Falsifiability

What would change this answer?

Later judgments or orders can extend the legal record, but they would not turn a court finding into a substitute for testing unrelated software. Evidence that a product claim expressly depends on the rejected identity claim would make the history directly material to that claim.
