What do low hashpower and reorganization history mean for security?

“BSV has suffered attacks and has less hashpower than BTC. Governance responses do not replace Nakamoto consensus.”
Short answer

The answer without the theater.

Lower economic hashpower can make proof-of-work reorganization attacks cheaper relative to a higher-hash chain using the same algorithm. Applications should model that risk explicitly. BSV also treats node rules and 'honest' participation as part of its response model. Critics are right that governance response is not the same thing as more hashpower; supporters are right that proof of work does not make an invalid transaction valid.

What is true

The objection's strongest ground

  • Proof-of-work security is economic and relative. Confirmation requirements should reflect current conditions and transaction value.
  • A valid-chain dispute, an invalid transaction, a double-spend attempt, and a policy response are not interchangeable events.
  • BSV's explicit node governance means a security analysis must include institutions and directive mechanisms as well as hashes and blocks.
What remains unresolved

Do not claim more than the evidence

  • No fixed confirmation number is safe for every value, threat model, or current network condition.
  • Calling attackers dishonest does not remove the engineering requirement to detect, price, and respond to reorganization risk.
Inspect it yourself

A better next move than arguing

  1. Monitor chainwork, miner distribution, stale blocks, reorganization alerts, and the policies of the services you depend on.
  2. Use Merkle proofs and transaction ancestry where appropriate, but do not confuse inclusion proof with an absolute settlement guarantee.
  3. Document operational responses before accepting high-value or irreversible actions.
Falsifiability

What would change this answer?

A material shift in hash economics, miner distribution, attack history, monitoring transparency, or rule enforcement should change the risk assessment quickly.
